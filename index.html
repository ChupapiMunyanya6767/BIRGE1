01 SECTION ONE · THE PROBLEM

A real concern.
An incomplete picture.

Loneliness is the gap between relationships a person wants and those they feel they have. It is not the same as living alone, depression or suicidal behaviour; merging them into one crisis statistic would be inaccurate.

AWhat counts as
“youth” here?

Kazakhstan’s Law on State Youth Policy defines young people as citizens aged 14 to 35. This project focuses on adults aged 18–34, a deliberate subset: it keeps the proposed service away from minors while staying within the broad policy category. This boundary scopes the app, not loneliness itself.[1]

The social-science question is not simply “Are young people lonely?” It is: How do social expectations, life transitions, unequal access to safe spaces, and platform design shape young adults’ chances of finding meaningful connection—and under what conditions could an opt-in recommendation system help? It asks who does emotional work, who can participate safely, and who remains invisible.

Social ties can offer everyday support. A 2025 nationwide adult survey found 5% had no close relative to discuss important matters with, and 5.3% had one.[2]

A survey of 2,065 students at three universities used a six-item loneliness scale. Loneliness was associated with burnout and lower financial well-being; housing conditions did not significantly distinguish loneliness. The cross-sectional, student sample cannot represent all young people or show what came first.[3]

Why not call this a “loneliness epidemic”?

We found no repeated, representative measure establishing a trend among Kazakhstan’s young adults. WHO estimates 15.8% globally, including 17.4% for ages 18–29 and 20.9% for adolescents; pooled international surveys are not Kazakhstan prevalence.[4]

THE PROBLEM WE CAN DEFEND

Not “Kazakhstan’s youth are all lonely.” The defensible problem is that some young people lack the kind of trusted, meaningful connection they want, while local evidence about the scale, distribution and causes remains incomplete.

A SERIOUS NATIONAL HEALTH SIGNAL · 2023

Self-harm ranks among Kazakhstan’s leading causes of death.

WHO’s Global Health Estimates rank it by crude death rate among 135 cause categories. These are modelled, all-age estimates—not registered death counts.[49]

THE SILENCE IS PART OF THE DATA

Who gets lost between the measures?

Age. The adult network survey lacks an 18–34 breakdown; the loneliness survey covers younger students. Neither estimates adult youth prevalence.

Place and language. Three universities do not represent non-students, rural youth or every region, even with Kazakh and Russian questionnaires.

Disclosure. Stigma may suppress reports of loneliness, suicidal thoughts, harassment or relationship needs; registries reflect what was recorded.

Measures. Few confidants is not loneliness; a screen is not diagnosis; attempts and deaths are different outcomes.

Missing. Repeated adult 18–34 loneliness measures, regional comparisons, local app-trust data, and evidence about what connections young people want.

Survey limits. KazISS reports 8,001 adults across 17 regions and three cities, but its summary lacks questionnaire, response-rate and weighting details. Treat it as a signal, not a census.[2]

03 SECTION THREE · CURRENT STATE OF RESEARCH

The app is not
an empty canvas.

Dating-app research follows three connected questions: how design shapes attention, who carries safety work, and how app culture reflects appearance and belonging back to users.

APPROACH 01THE INTERFACE

More options do not automatically create better connection.

Among 464 dating-app users aged 16–25, excessive swiping was associated with choice overload, upward social comparison and fear of being single.[10]

Narr and Luong describe how repetitive app interactions can create a mood of boredom that feeds ghosting and flaking: algorithmic ranking shapes not only who users see, but how they experience the search.[11]

In a 440-user study, perceived barriers of use, risk and tradition each lowered intention to adopt online dating apps. A favorable attitude softened the usage barrier, but not perceived risk or tradition—so a simpler interface alone may not settle privacy or social-norm concerns.[12]

DESIGN TAKEAWAY

Reduce endless browsing, show why two profiles fit, and let both people decline without penalty. Treat adoption as a question for local research.

APPROACH 02THE SAFETY WORK

Safety is often work pushed onto the person at risk.

Women in Vares’s interviews described safety practices as giving them some control online, while meeting men in person could remain shaped by fear of rape and murder. Vares argues that safety work can also reproduce rape-culture scripts that make women responsible for managing men’s violence.[13]

Phan and colleagues connect dating-app-enabled crime with physical and psychological effects, and with sensitive information exposed through cybersecurity failures. Their review points toward layered platform safeguards, not safety advice aimed only at individual users.[14]

Qu and colleagues found sexting victimization more common among U.S. than Chinese college dating-app users. In the Chinese sample, rape-myth acceptance predicted victimization; the pattern differed across national settings, genders, ages and relationship status.[15]

!

“Report and block” buttons cannot stop a determined hostile user from joining. Safety needs trained human review, privacy by design, response standards, appeals and independent testing—not a badge or a promise.

APPROACH 03THE USER EXPERIENCE

A match score is not a measure of someone’s worth.

Among women in a study of 118 Australian dating-app users, perceived success consistently predicted appearance-based rejection sensitivity, body appreciation, weight-control behaviour and acceptance of cosmetic surgery. Men showed different body-image patterns related to app experiences.[16]

Clicks, messages, dates, friendships and reduced loneliness are different outcomes. Engagement can rise while wellbeing worsens; evaluate reported connection and safety, not only matches or time spent.

THE GAP THIS PROJECT SPEAKS TO

We need Kazakhstan-based evidence on adults’ loneliness and support networks, local attitudes toward matching, and how friendship-oriented introductions might work alongside romance. This project sketches a testable, privacy-minded pilot; it does not fill that gap by itself.

WHAT WE KNOW / WHAT WE DO NOT

We know: recommendation systems can alter what people see; swiping can be tiring for some users; safety and body-image concerns deserve attention.

We do not know: whether an AI matching service would reduce loneliness, improve mental health, prevent suicide or be trusted by young adults in Kazakhstan. Those outcomes require direct, locally designed evaluation.

COMMERCIAL DATING SITE · AUSTRALIA / 2013 DEPLOYMENT

A deployed people-to-people recommender

At a dating site with nearly half a million active users, Krzywicki et al. deployed profile, collaborative-filtering and rule-based recommendations. After launch, 61% clicked suggestions and 33% messaged them.[20]

DATING PLATFORM · TAIWAN · FIELD EXPERIMENT, 2023

Optimise for mutual matches, not popularity

Chen et al.’s field experiment tested two-sided matching on a Taiwanese dating platform. It reduced recommendation inequality, improved predictive accuracy and produced more mutual matches than comparison algorithms.[21]

816serious relationships reportedofficial cumulative count, August 2026
TOKYO METROPOLITAN GOVERNMENT · TOKYO ENMUSUBI

AI matching as a public service

Tokyo Enmusubi uses an assessment, user conditions, identity and single-status documents, and an online interview. By August 2026, government reported around 16,000 registered users, 816 serious relationships and 302 marriages. These unbenchmarked administrative counts cannot show causation; unlike BIRGE, the service targets marriage.[22]

02
₸

Who pays; who profits?

At ₸4,000/month, 7,605 average payers (1.52% of 500,000 MAU) gross about ₸1m/day over a year: ₸1m × 365 ÷ (₸4,000 × 12). This is gross revenue. The known-price floor alone equals 3,440–4,965 payers; fees, taxes and unpriced work raise break-even. Ads are unpriced; never sell profile data.

03
✳

Labor behind the model

Five people cover one continuous review seat. Other workers label data, support users, handle appeals, maintain systems and review Kazakh/Russian content. Fund training and rotation; let humans override flags. Size the team from pilot report volumes.

04
⌂

Governance and misuse

The governance questions are who controls BIRGE, where data stays and how users challenge results. The AI Law sets risk duties and prohibits social scoring; the Digital Code provides review rights in some cases. Store personal data in Kazakhstan. Separate identity and profiles, log access, obtain eGov approval and consent. Linked ID/chat records risk surveillance or targeting. Humans decide bans and police referrals.[24][17][25]

No public IIN, document image or “verified = safe” badge.

05
♡

Data ethics and consent

Do not collect precise location; never infer whom users seek. Use contextual ads, never targeting based on chats, seeking preferences or mental-health data. Screen images, blur uncertainty and let users choose to open; hide reported text in the reporter’s chat. Humans decide restrictions.

Offer deletion and a non-eGov path; test privacy, language access and targeted-abuse risks locally.

THE VERDICT · FOR NOWPILOT ONLY · FIVE GATES

Only test it under
these conditions.

BIRGE is not suicide prevention; evidence cannot show that it reduces loneliness or suicide in Kazakhstan. 500,000 MAU is a scale scenario, not a first cohort.

Start only after gates 1–4 and full funding. Continue only if gate 5 passes independent review. Otherwise, do not start, or pause, repair or close.

  1. Independent evaluation firstCo-design locally; pre-register measures of wanted connection, trust and harm. Set pilot size and success thresholds before recruitment.
  2. Privacy and safety reviewedIndependent legal, security and abuse-risk review; minimal adult check, non-eGov route, no stored ID/face copies, funded human report review.
  3. Full-year costs securedWritten quotes and 12 months’ funding for staff, hosting, support, evaluation, privacy and incident response. Ads and subscriptions are not committed revenue.
  4. Purpose limits are publicName the operator; publish contracts, data rules and reports; log access; store data in Kazakhstan; never target ads from chats or sensitive preferences.
  5. Continue only if it worksRenew only if independent reviews meet pre-set connection, safety, privacy and funding criteria. Serious harm, failed safeguards or no funding means pause or close.

+ MAKING THE PROCESS VISIBLE

The AI helped make
this page. It is not the author.

AI’s role: AI helped find and check sources, draft the narrative, build the site and visualisations, and calculate labelled comparisons. No observations were invented.

What I accepted, changed and rejected: I kept an adult 18–34, one-year pilot in Astana and Almaty; widened romance to friendship and romance; and removed swiping. I set ten free daily introductions and a ₸4,000/month subscription for up to 30 total. Ads may help cover costs, but their income is unknown. The 500,000 MAU figure is a scale scenario, not a forecast or first cohort. I supplied the WHO 2023 crude-rate files and accepted the self-harm ranking with its modelled-estimate caveat. I replaced IIN/document/face collection with optional approved verification and a non-eGov route; age-only eGov verification is unconfirmed. I chose AI image screening and uncertain-image blurring, report-triggered text review, and human decisions on bans and police referrals. I added an optional deletion question and support contacts for all ages. I asked AI to compare BIRGE with Bumble and Tinder; I kept the dated price figures with their billing-period limits, and changed “better” into a conditional design claim. I changed the verdict to a pilot only after independent evaluation, privacy and safety review, secured full-year costs, public purpose limits, and continuation criteria. I rejected suicide-prevention and loneliness-to-birth-rate claims, and any promise of perfect safety. Queer safety is a privacy and misuse consideration, not the project’s main frame.
